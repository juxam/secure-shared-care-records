\# Problem Statement



Healthcare systems increasingly depend on timely access to patient information across organisational, professional and technological boundaries. In routine, urgent and emergency care, clinicians often require access to medication history, allergies, diagnoses, test results, discharge summaries, referrals, care plans and previous encounters to make safe and informed decisions. When patient records are fragmented across GP surgeries, ambulance services, emergency departments, urgent treatment centres, walk-in clinics, secondary care providers and specialist services, clinicians may be forced to make decisions with incomplete information. This can delay treatment, increase duplication, weaken continuity of care and place additional burden on patients who may have to repeatedly explain their medical history across different care settings.



The movement toward shared care records and single patient records seeks to address this fragmentation by enabling authorised professionals to access relevant patient information at the point of care. In principle, wider access to complete or near-complete patient records can improve clinical decision-making, medication safety, care coordination and patient experience. This is especially important in urgent and emergency contexts where patients may be unconscious, distressed, confused or unable to provide a reliable history. It is also important in routine and specialist care, where clinicians need accurate information about referrals, prior diagnoses, test results, medication changes and discharge instructions.



However, wider patient-record accessibility also creates a significant security and privacy challenge. The same infrastructure that improves information availability also expands the number of users, organisations, systems, devices, suppliers and access contexts through which sensitive health information may be viewed, transferred, misused or compromised. Patient medical records may contain highly sensitive information relating to mental health, reproductive health, safeguarding, long-term conditions, medication, genetic information, social circumstances and previous care encounters. When access is extended across multiple care settings, the risk landscape becomes broader and more complex.



The security risks include unauthorised access, weak identity and access management, compromised credentials, insider misuse, excessive emergency access, mobile-device exposure, supplier-side compromise, poor audit monitoring and inaccurate or outdated data being reused in clinical decisions. Privacy risks include over-disclosure, weak data minimisation, access to unrelated sensitive history, unclear respect for patient preferences, lack of patient-facing transparency and uncertainty about who has accessed a record and why. These risks are not only technical. They are socio-technical because they arise from the interaction between healthcare workflows, professional roles, clinical urgency, system design, organisational governance, supplier relationships and patient expectations.



The central problem is therefore not simply whether patient records should be shared. The more important question is how shared access can be made necessary, proportionate, secure, auditable, transparent and clinically useful. Different care contexts require different levels of access. A paramedic responding to an emergency, an emergency department doctor managing acute deterioration, a GP reviewing a discharge summary, a walk-in clinic clinician treating a minor condition and a specialist consultant assessing a referral may all have legitimate information needs. However, they may not require the same depth of access to the patient’s full medical history. A secure and privacy-preserving shared-record model must therefore account for role, context, urgency, sensitivity, care relationship, access justification, auditability and organisational accountability.



This project investigates the security and privacy implications of cross-provider access to complete patient medical records for direct care. It focuses on GP surgeries, ambulance services, urgent care, emergency care, walk-in clinics, secondary care providers, specialist providers and related community care interfaces. The project aims to map relevant data flows, identify key security and privacy risks, analyse existing governance mechanisms and develop a context-aware framework for secure and privacy-preserving shared record access. The contribution of the project lies in addressing the practical tension between complete information for care delivery and controlled access for confidentiality, trust, accountability and compliance.
